What 80% tread means

Most of the tire, still there.

Tread is the part of the tire that actually touches the road. Its grooves give water somewhere to go, which is what keeps the rubber gripping the pavement instead of floating on top of it. As a tire wears, those grooves get shallower and wet-weather grip is the first thing to suffer.

Tread depth is measured in 32nds of an inch. Most new passenger tires start somewhere around 10/32 to 11/32, and 2/32 is the widely used minimum before a tire is considered worn out. A tire with 80% of its tread remaining has used only a small slice of its working life, and it still has deep grooves for rainy Florida roads.

For you as a driver, that means a used tire that rides, grips and drains water much like a new one, at a price that makes sense when you need to replace one or two tires, match a set, or keep an older car rolling without overspending.

Buying used, the smart way

What a careful used-tire check looks at.

Not every used tire deserves a second life. These are the things any good tire inspection covers, and the reason our line starts at 80% tread.

Tread depth

Measured across the whole width of the tread, not just one spot, so you know the depth is real everywhere the tire meets the road.

Even wear

Wear that is heavier on one edge or in the center can point to a past alignment or pressure problem. An even wear pattern is what you want to see.

Sidewalls

Cuts, bulges and deep cracking in the sidewall are deal breakers. The sidewall carries the load and cannot be safely patched.

Punctures

The tread is checked for nails, screws and old repairs. Any damage is noted before a tire ever goes on a car.

Age

The last four digits of the DOT code on the sidewall show the week and year the tire was made. Rubber ages even when tread is deep, so age matters too.

Bead and fit

The bead is the edge that seals against the wheel. A clean, undamaged bead plus proper mounting and balancing is what gives you a smooth, leak-free ride.

Why brakes matter

Your car will tell you.

Brakes wear gradually, which makes it easy to get used to small changes until they become big ones. The good news is that most brake problems announce themselves well before they become dangerous, through sounds, feel and the way the car behaves when you stop.

Catching those signs early usually means a simpler, less expensive repair. Waiting can turn a routine pad replacement into a bigger job, because worn pads can start damaging the parts around them.

If anything on the list below sounds familiar, give us a call. We will look at it honestly and tell you what needs doing now and what can wait.

Warning signs

Five signs your brakes need attention.

01

Squealing

A high-pitched squeal when you brake is often the built-in wear indicator on the pads, a small metal tab designed to make noise when the pads are getting thin.

02

Grinding

A harsh grinding or scraping sound can mean the pads are worn down to metal. That metal-on-metal contact can damage the rotors quickly, so do not wait on this one.

03

Soft pedal

A pedal that feels spongy or sinks closer to the floor than usual can point to air or moisture in the brake fluid, a leak, or other hydraulic issues. Get it checked promptly.

04

Pulling

If the car drifts to one side when you brake, one side may be gripping harder than the other. Uneven pad wear or a sticking caliper are common causes.

05

Vibration

A pulsing pedal or a shaking steering wheel while braking often means the rotors have worn unevenly. It is uncomfortable, and it reduces how smoothly you stop.

Maintenance tips

Small habits that save big repairs.

A little attention goes a long way. These general habits help any car last longer and run safer.

Check tire pressure

Check monthly with a gauge, using the pressure on your door jamb sticker rather than the number on the tire. Correct pressure helps grip, fuel use and tire life.

Watch your tread

Shallow tread hurts braking most in the rain. Uneven wear is also an early sign that something else, like alignment or pressure, needs a look.

Follow your manual

Your owner's manual lists service intervals for oil, fluids and inspections built for your specific vehicle. It is the best schedule to follow.

Mind the dash lights

Warning lights are your car asking for attention. A brake or tire pressure light is worth checking soon rather than later.

Listen and feel

New noises, smells, shakes or a change in how the car drives are often the first signs of trouble. Catching them early keeps repairs small.

Rotate and balance

Rotating tires helps them wear evenly, and balanced wheels keep the ride smooth and protect suspension parts from extra vibration.
